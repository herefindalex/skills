# Validation Questions and Follow-up Updates

## One question tests one important interpretation

Usually select three to five questions that could most change understanding of the work, or fewer when evidence is insufficient. For each, identify the hypothesis, the concrete example requested, and how different answers would revise the interpretation.

Each selected question has three required parts:

- The particular decision, outcome, or unknown it tests.
- A recent actual example from the target team, including who made the decision and what happened. If no such event occurred, ask how the responsibility is currently exercised and retain the lack of an example as a limitation.
- Two plausible answer patterns and the different revisions they would imply. State these consequences in the report; do not put a preferred answer into the interviewer's mouth.

Prefer a short question tied to the strongest unresolved hypothesis over a generic question about process, culture, or authority. A peer's incident or a leadership change suggests what to ask the target team about; it does not establish that the target has the same problem.

For a company with little operating evidence, establish whether the described use is paid production, a pilot, or exploration, and request a recent customer outcome before inferring operational maturity. For a role expected to take responsibility from founders, ask which decisions move to the role, which remain with founders, and at what agreed checkpoint; use a recent decision to distinguish actual delegation from a future intention.

| Question | Problem | More discriminating question |
|---|---|---|
| Is your culture good? | Invites a vague answer. | Who resolved the most recent priority conflict, and where would this role participate? |
| Do the founders refuse to delegate? | Embeds an unsupported assumption. | Which decisions currently handled by founders will move to this role in six months? Is there a current example? |
| Does the system have extensive technical debt? | Diagnoses a problem without evidence. | What technical improvement was most recently deferred? Who made the tradeoff, and how did it affect delivery? |

Adapt questions to the target JD instead of copying the whole table. Avoid private, discriminatory, or unrelated questions.

## Connect answers to revisions

For a hypothesis that a manager will assume architecture decisions: if architecture remains with a separate Staff team, narrow the hypothesis to delivery coordination and people responsibilities. If an answer provides an example of independent decisions, increase support for authority within that scope. Neither answer alone makes the company good or bad.

A restated vision does not establish transferred authority. Interview answers are the speaker's statements, not automatically independent evidence of company behavior. [T10/T14]

## New information

Update affected items only, using four simple fields: original interpretation, new information and its type, revised interpretation, and remaining question. Explicitly withdraw statements whose support has disappeared instead of merely appending a note to the original conclusion. [T14]

Prioritize sources by applicability, time, and directness, rather than always treating interview answers as superior to public evidence. Preserve conflicting accounts or different scopes and ask for clarification.

## Missing prior research

Use a report already available in the conversation without requesting it again. Across conversations, if the host has not supplied prior material, do not claim to have loaded it. Ask for the passages or key hypotheses the user wants updated, then make a focused revision. [R04]

If the original assessment is absent, make one concise request for the affected passages or hypotheses and any missing company identity and exact role or title. Acknowledge the new information and label its source, then wait for that context before producing a revised assessment. An isolated interview statement cannot establish which original conclusion should change. Request only missing fields; a report already present in the conversation supplies them.

Keep private material relevant to the work. Use labels such as "the interviewer" or "the candidate" and omit contact details, unrelated identifiers, and salary figures unless the user specifically requests analysis of those details. Authorization to analyze supplied material does not require repeating every field in the answer.

Do not automatically store the report in external tools, send questions, or contact the company. A public report must not include interview quotations or personal information the user has not authorized sharing. Do not send private new information to external search. [T14/T17]
